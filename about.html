Prologue · Who I am

Hi, I’m Minh.

I’m from Vietnam. I’m non-binary and trans, and I’m gay. I’m also a senior at Dartmouth, double majoring in Computer Science and Mathematics.

I’m a person of faith, too. I’m Buddhist, and lately I’ve been growing closer to God and to Christianity, through Bible study at Dartmouth’s Tucker Center and going to church. And to stay healthy inside and out, I’ve been doing Pilates and yoga.

Academically, I love AI and machine learning. Professionally, I love finance, business, and trading. Passionately, I love fashion and modeling. And I have a big heart: I love helping people.

So most of what I’ve built has a person on the other end of it: the Deaf community in Vietnam, patients waiting for care, elders and children who need someone watching out for them—and now, fashionistas looking for a space of their own.

My motivation is my mom. You’ll meet her in Chapter Six.

Chapter One · Experience, 2021–2025

Built for people.

Three projects, three communities: a sign-language translator, faster access to care, and cameras that call for help.

Scene 01 · Vietnam · 2021–2023

Signtegrate

My high-school start-up

A real-time Vietnamese Sign Language translator. I collected and annotated 394 sign videos, tracked hands and body with a MediaPipe pipeline, and trained a DD-Net model that recognizes signs in under a second, at 70.1% accuracy.

First runner-up, SV.STARTUP 2021, Vietnam’s national student start-up competition

Made for the Deaf community in Vietnam

Scene 02 · Hanover, NH · 2024

Patient-access research

Dartmouth · FYREE fellow, then E. E. Just summer intern

I cleaned and queried 11,000+ anonymized patient-flow and scheduling records from five Dartmouth-Hitchcock clinics, then built discrete-event simulations in AnyLogic and SimPy to test new scheduling strategies. In simulation, they were projected to cut average waits by 25% and raise 10-day patient access from 14–28% to 55%.

Made for patients waiting for care

Scene 03 · Hanoi · 2025

FPT Software

AI Computer Vision Engineer Intern

I worked on a camera-based safety system designed to spot falls—especially for elders and children—and alert firefighters, police, and hospitals. I crawled 10,000+ images across five poses, auto-labeled a 100,000-image dataset (70% less manual labeling), and served a real-time vision-language model on a Jetson Nano with 40% lower latency.

Made for elders, children, and the responders who reach them

Also: I diagnosed a NAT port-mapping issue between a WebRTC client and NVIDIA Omniverse Isaac Sim running in Docker.

Chapter Two · Finance, 2025–2026

Follow the money.

A valuation at Tuck, then a summer in alternative credit at Magnetar Capital.

Scene 04 · Hanover, NH · December 2025

Tuck Business Bridge Program

AECOM valuation · Tuck School of Business

My team built a three-statement model and a DCF for AECOM, projecting $22.6B in revenue and $1.1B in EBIT by 2029: a $109.56 base-case share price, about 11% upside, which we presented to executives and faculty. I led the AI and proprietary-software analysis, turning adoption scenarios into the margin, CapEx, working-capital, and free-cash-flow assumptions.

Made for executives and faculty at Tuck

Scene 05 · Evanston, IL · Summer 2026

Magnetar Capital

Alternative Credit & Fixed Income Summer Analyst

Fundamental credit

I modeled financial statement forecasts and conducted qualitative credit analysis of high-yield automotive issuers to evaluate free cash flow, interest coverage, refinancing needs, liquidity, and default risk. I benchmarked CDS spreads against those modeled credit fundamentals to test whether market pricing reflected default risk, then presented the team’s recommendation to senior investment professionals.

Thematic diligence

I partnered with a team to assess near- and long-term demand and credit risk across energy infrastructure tied to AI power needs, weighing financial strength, regulatory risk, and contract concentration. I synthesized permitting and execution-risk diligence into a case for cash-flow-backed, asset-based investments.

Company names, positions, and recommendations from my internship stay confidential.

Chapter Three · The market I’m watching

Fashion sells the dream. Beauty pays the bills.

Ask me what market I’m watching, and I’ll say fashion and beauty deals. L’Oréal, beauty’s leader, is outgrowing luxury fashion; the groups that need cash are selling; and the buyers with the strongest balance sheets are writing the biggest checks. I follow each deal from the headline to the balance sheet: who sold, who paid, how it was financed, and what it did to credit. It’s the same trail I’m building MyFashionSpace’s Deal Room and Credit & Bond Monitor to follow.

Personal research notes · Built from public sources only · As of September 2026 · Views my own, not any employer’s · Not investment advice

July 7, 2026 Coty agrees to hand its Gucci beauty license back to Kering a year early, for about $400M Source: sec.gov

  1. 01

    The split

    In the first half of 2026, L’Oréal grew 6.8% like-for-like with a record first-half operating margin of 21.3%, and US prestige and mass beauty sales both rose 7% (Circana). Over the same six months, LVMH’s Fashion & Leather Goods sales fell 1% organically, turning positive only in the second quarter, and Bain expects personal luxury to grow just 2–4% this year.

  2. 02

    The sellers

    Groups that need cash are selling. Kering sold its beauty arm to L’Oréal for €4.0B in cash; with a Milan property sale, that helped cut its net debt from €8.0B to €3.3B in six months. Capri sold Versace to Prada and used the cash to pay down debt, taking net debt from $1.4B to $222M in a year. Even LVMH, which doesn’t need the money, sold Marc Jacobs.

  3. 03

    The buyer’s edge

    The strongest balance sheet writes the check. Rated AA by S&P and Aa1 by Moody’s, L’Oréal funded part of the deal with a €3B bond, including a 10-year at 3.375%. Coty went the other way. Even after selling KKR its Wella stake for $750M upfront, its net debt stood at 3.4 times adjusted EBITDA in June, and it’s reviewing its mass color-cosmetics and Brazil businesses, with decisions due by the end of 2026.

  4. 04

    What’s really for sale

    L’Oréal bought Creed outright, plus 50-year fragrance and beauty licenses for Bottega Veneta and Balenciaga; Gucci’s license is set to start July 1, 2027, pending regulatory approvals. To me, a 50-year license reads less like a brand deal and more like a marriage: Kering keeps the brands and collects royalties, while L’Oréal makes and sells their fragrance and beauty for half a century.

  5. 05

    The fault line

    Leverage decides who buys and who restructures. Saks Global funded its purchase of Neiman Marcus partly with $2.2B of 11% secured notes. About a year after closing, following a distressed exchange and a missed interest payment, it filed for Chapter 11. On June 26, it emerged as Exemplar Luxury Group, controlled by its bondholders, with its debt cut by nearly 75%.

  6. 06

    The IPO I’m watching

    KKR-controlled Wella, owner of OPI and ghd, filed on August 31 to list on the NYSE, and its September 23 amendment still leaves the price range blank. It plans to pay off its roughly $2.3B term loan, about 4.4 times its adjusted EBITDA, with IPO proceeds and new loans. After Olaplex went from a $21 IPO in 2021 to a $2.06-a-share buyout by Henkel, Wella’s price will show me whether investors will pay up for leveraged beauty again.

My view: over the next year, more deals will follow this script: owners carrying heavy debt sell to buyers with stronger balance sheets, and at the top end, long licenses stand in for outright sales. What would change my mind: L’Oréal’s growth sliding back toward luxury’s low single digits, a top-rated buyer losing its rating to win a deal, Wella pricing so well that more leveraged owners list instead of selling, or a fashion house that’s growing again taking beauty back in-house.

Chapter Six · Motivation

The closet she opened for me.

My mom is hard-working, ambitious, and so caring. In her twenties, she was the CFO of a tech start-up, before she gave her career up for our family.

The day before my flight back to college, she took me to a closet she had kept tucked away for decades. It was full of her clothes from her own college years in the 2000s. She had great fashion sense. She told me to pick whatever I wanted for college.

I was so emotional. Ever since coming out as non-binary and trans, it has felt ten times harder to navigate life and acceptance back home in Vietnam, where many people still hold traditional, conservative views.

I know I’ll always have her beside me, and I know how much she has sacrificed for me to be who I am today. That’s what drives me—in AI, in finance, and on the runway. Someday, I’m going to take her to places she’s never been, so she never has to save up for a vacation again.
